# Findings on the live Explore page (V5.6)

Copied from the public page at natural-experiment-scanner.com/viz/en/synthesize.html as of 2026-10-23. Counts quoted from that page come from the refresh of 2026-10-22 (155 designs); the tables in `results/` use the newer refresh of 2026-10-23 (170 designs). All findings are exploratory. A study is one policy rollout across US states. The numbers come from the result files in `results/l3/` and `results/l2_*`; the first finding is re-checked by `tools/check_headlines.py`, and the code that produced them is in `scripts/l3_meta/`.


## When and where policies work

- **Where the gap before adoption was larger, the change after it was larger too** Arithmetic alone would give the opposite pattern. Slope of the change after adoption on the gap between adopting and not-yet-adopting states the year before adoption: +0.33, against −0.20 from arithmetic alone (3.9 standard deviations apart); it stays between 3.0 and 4.2 when any one of 34 policy groups is left out; clearest for earnings and public finance, absent for crime and traffic; 50 studies, 106 outcome series.
- **Of the effects that show up, none faded** Two thirds held and one third grew. Of 64 studies followed at least six years, 46 show no clear effect in years 0 to 2 or years 3 to 5; of the 18 that do, 0 faded, 12 held and 6 grew, five of them health laws.
- **Early and late adopters of a law show no clear difference in results** Order of adoption shows no pattern in how well a law worked. Late adopters did better in 31 of 60 studies (52%), a coin flip; the median gap is +0.03 in units of each study’s noise, inside the −0.53 to +0.54 that chance gives when the order is shuffled; an order effect of about half a unit or more would have shown.
- **Party control at adoption shows no clear link to a law’s measured effect** The same holds for how liberal the state was. Unified Democratic versus unified Republican control: −0.75 in units of each study’s noise, inside the −1.33 to +1.26 that chance gives when labels are shuffled (23 studies); more versus less liberal states: −0.42, inside −0.68 to +0.68 (46 studies); gaps smaller than about 1.3 and 0.7 would not show.
- **Few of the laws we could check clearly delivered what was promised** Most show no detectable change, which mostly means the data cannot tell. 16 laws whose promised outcome, as recalled by an AI model, is the outcome we measured: 2 moved as promised, 12 showed no detectable change and 2 moved the opposite way.

## How states make policy

- **Some states lead again and again, but their laws show no clear difference in effects** The habitual leaders are richer, more urban and more liberal states. California, Minnesota, Connecticut, Rhode Island, New York, Idaho, Massachusetts and Washington tend to adopt early. The ranking from half the policies predicts the ranking from the other half (0.46, against 0.29 or less by chance; 375 policies). In 34 studies, cohorts from 13 habitual-leader states differ from other states by −0.05 in units of each study’s noise, inside the −1.03 to +1.14 that chance gives.
- **Laws are not timed to election years or downturns** Laws that began in a downturn show no clear difference in how often they delivered. 45.2% of adoptions fall in a lower-house election year against 46.1% expected by chance, and 36.3% follow a weak income year against 36.0% (418 policies); national waves are no stronger than chance; the downturn laws’ delivery share is −5.4 points, inside the −11.9 to +12.2 that chance gives (35 studies).
- **How fast a law spreads shows no clear link to how big its effect is** The link changes sign between policy fields. Correlation of spread speed and effect size 0.14, inside the −0.36 to +0.35 that chance gives when labels are shuffled; positive in four policy fields and negative in two; 51 groups of evaluated laws.
- **A neighbor’s improvement after adopting shows no clear link to a state adopting sooner** A placebo that looks two years ahead gives as large an effect. Effect per typical annual change 0.05 (95% interval −0.08 to 0.18), inside the −0.04 to 0.12 that chance gives when labels are shuffled; the two-years-ahead placebo gives 0.08; 37 studies, 304 adoptions, 5,562 state-years.
- **Within a few topics, laws often reach the same states close in time** So one law’s measured effect may carry its partners’ effects. In civil rights, energy, government operations and transportation, laws reach the same states within three years of each other more often than chance. 53% of 375 laws have a close partner (same states, within three years; 44% without likely duplicates); the evaluated laws we could match are no different: 8 of 12 (7 without likely duplicates).

## Which policies can be evaluated

- **Every hand-run design reached an estimate; most automatic runs did not** Checked roughly, designs without an estimate are about as often positive as those with one. 155 designs: 81 ended with an estimate and 74 did not. 48 were run by hand, all estimated; 97 were run automatically, 33 estimated; 10 more stopped early. Among the automatic runs, policy field and adoption era show no clear link to getting an estimate. A rough before-and-after comparison of adopting with other states on 54 of the 74 without an estimate gives 24 with a positive sign, against 35 of the 81 with one.

## Technical checks

- **With each paper’s own method, most published results reproduce within 10%** Where we differ, our estimate is closer to zero in 13 of 17. 36 of 53 papers, starting with the easiest data to get, differ from the published figure by no more than 10% of its size, or of its standard error if that is larger.
- **Adopting states differ from the rest in party, ideology, income and urbanization** So comparing adopters with non-adopters picks up those differences too. The gap is on average 2.84 standard deviations larger than when the same adoptions are handed to states at random (95% interval 1.50 to 4.18); 63 studies, 45 policy groups; in 34 of the 63 the gap alone is beyond chance, against 3.15 expected by chance.
- **States adopt a policy faster when the party that favors it is in charge** The link shows up from 1990 and is not seen before. Each step (from the other party in control, to split control, to this party in control) goes with 19% higher yearly odds that a state adopts for the first time (95% interval 10% to 28%); per step the odds ratio is 1.39 in 1990–2016 and 0.94 in 1961–1989; 166 policies, 4,309 adoptions.
- **Estimates for policies that reach many of the same states tend to move together** Results that share states count for less than the same number of independent ones. Pairs of studies that share more of their adopting states move together more (slope +2.31 on the share of states in common, 95% interval 0.43 to 4.19); 226 study pairs, 28 studies.
